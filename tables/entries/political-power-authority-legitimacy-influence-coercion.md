# Political Power, Authority, Legitimacy, Influence, and Coercion

Status: candidate anchor entry

## Orientation

Political power is a capacity to shape political conduct, conditions, agendas, or outcomes. Political authority is a claimed or justified standing to issue and possibly enforce public directives. Legitimacy is descriptive acceptance or normative justification under an explicit account. Influence changes beliefs or conduct without necessarily issuing authoritative directives. Coercion pressures conduct through a threat, force, or constrained alternative.

## Sense table

| Sense | Governing question | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `political-power` | Which actor can shape which political conduct, agenda, conditions, or outcomes through what mechanism? | political outcome-shaping capacity |
| `political-authority` | Who has which standing to issue or enforce which public directives over whom? | directive-issuing political standing |
| `political-legitimacy` | Which institution, decision, or exercise of power is accepted or justified under which descriptive or normative account? | political acceptability/justification status |
| `political-influence` | Who alters whose beliefs, preferences, agenda, or conduct through what nonconclusive mechanism? | political shaping relation |
| `political-coercion` | Who pressures whom to do or avoid what through which threat, force, or constrained alternative? | pressure-backed conduct relation |

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Power vs. influence | influence is one power mechanism | capacity to shape outcomes vs. a shaping relation/channel |
| Power vs. authority | authority may confer power | capacity/effect vs. standing to direct |
| Authority vs. legitimacy | legitimacy may justify authority | claimed or held standing vs. acceptance/justification status |
| Legitimacy vs. legality | law may support legitimacy | descriptive/normative justification vs. legal status |
| Influence vs. coercion | both alter conduct | shaping without necessary threat vs. threat/force-constrained choice |
| Authority vs. obligation | authority may generate duties under some accounts | directive standing vs. subject's duty |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

Generic influence/causation, organizational authority, deontic obligation, consent, and justice retain their owners. This Table stops before declaring a regime legitimate, a use of force justified, a duty to obey, unlawful coercion, or a strategy for political manipulation.

## Selection procedure

1. Name actor, target, conduct/outcome, political setting, mechanism, and time.
2. Select power capacity, authority standing, legitimacy status, influence relation, or coercion relation.
3. State whether legitimacy is descriptive or normative and name its account.
4. Separate legality, justice, effectiveness, acceptance, and obligation.
5. Record alternatives, resistance, enforcement, evidence, and contested attribution.

## Reference Delta

| Reference form | Typical contribution | Lexicon delta |
|---|---|---|
| Political philosophy | analyzes authority, legitimacy, and obligation | keeps descriptive and normative claims separate |
| Political science | studies power, influence, acceptance, and institutions | requires mechanism and evidence rather than labels |
| Legal authority | allocates competence and enforcement | does not alone establish moral legitimacy |

## Failure signs

- effectiveness or obedience proves legitimacy;
- holding office proves unlimited authority;
- popularity, legality, justice, and legitimacy collapse;
- every influence is coercion or every coercion removes agency entirely;
- political power is inferred from outcomes without a mechanism;
- the Table judges a live regime or supplies manipulation guidance.
