# Syntax, Grammar, Acceptability, Grammaticality, and Usage

Status: candidate anchor entry

## Orientation

Syntax is the organization and dependency of linguistic expressions under a structural analysis. Grammar is a broader system or account of patterns and constraints in a language or variety, potentially including morphology, syntax, phonology, and other levels. Acceptability is a speaker's or community's graded response to an expression under elicited or natural conditions. Grammaticality is conformity to a stated grammar or analysis. Usage is attested or conventional language practice in a population, genre, register, setting, and period.

## Sense table

| Sense | Governing question | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `syntax` | Which structural relations organize which expression under which analysis? | structural subsystem |
| `grammar` | Which system or descriptive account governs which language/variety and levels? | language-system account |
| `acceptability` | Who judges which expression how, under which prompt, context, and conditions? | response evidence |
| `grammaticality` | Does which expression conform to which stated grammar or analysis? | analysis-relative conformity |
| `usage` | Which form or pattern is attested or conventional for which population, register, genre, and time? | situated language practice |

## Root factorization

```text
grammar-evidence-use
  := language or variety, community, modality, expression, context, and time
   x syntactic units, relations, dependencies, constraints, and analysis
   x grammar scope, levels, descriptive or prescriptive purpose, and version
   x acceptability respondent, task, scale, prompt, order, context, and variation
   x grammaticality criterion, derivation or constraint result, and competing analyses
   x usage corpus, population, register, genre, frequency, distribution, and change
   x sampling, annotation, authority, evidence strength, uncertainty, and provenance
```

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Syntax vs. grammar | syntax is part of many grammars | structural subsystem vs. broader system/account |
| Acceptability vs. grammaticality | both evaluate expressions | observed/elicited response vs. conformity under a grammar |
| Usage vs. acceptability | use can affect judgments | attested/conventional practice vs. respondent evaluation |
| Descriptive vs. prescriptive grammar | both state patterns or constraints | account of practice/system vs. recommended norm |
| Frequency vs. grammaticality | frequent forms may be licensed | distribution in data vs. analysis-relative status |
| Standard usage vs. language as a whole | standards are varieties | institutionally privileged convention vs. full variation |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

Morpheme/Word Formation owns morphology; Lexeme/Clause/Sentence owns unit routing; Register/Variety owns situational varieties; Norm/Convention owns general normativity. This Table stops before diagnosing speakers, ranking varieties as inherently superior, treating one theory as universal, or inferring grammaticality from frequency or acceptability alone.

## Selection procedure

1. Declare language/variety, population, modality, expression, context, period, and purpose.
2. Choose structural relation, grammar account, respondent judgment, theory-relative conformity, or usage question.
3. Record theory/guideline/version, data source, sampling, elicitation design, register, and uncertainty.
4. Keep descriptive findings, prescriptive recommendations, judgments, corpus counts, and formal analyses distinct.
5. Preserve variation and competing analyses; route word formation and discourse organization to their owners.

## Reference Delta

| Reference form | Typical contribution | Lexicon delta |
|---|---|---|
| formal grammar | derives or licenses structures | makes grammaticality conditional on the declared grammar |
| acceptability study | collects participant responses | requires task, sample, context, scale, and uncertainty |
| corpus/usage guide | records distributions or conventions | separates attestation, frequency, recommendation, and system claim |

## Failure signs

- acceptable, grammatical, frequent, standard, and preferred mean the same thing;
- one speaker judgment is generalized to a language;
- absence from a corpus proves ungrammaticality;
- a prescriptive rule is presented as an exhaustive description;
- dialect, register, genre, period, task, or theory is omitted;
- social prestige is mistaken for structural superiority.
